How Long Does It Take to Build a Data Center?

The building takes 18 to 30 months. Getting power to it takes more than four years. Why the interconnection queue, not the contractor, sets the delivery date.

PRIME_DOMAIN_CAPITAL_RESEARCH · 2026-11-19 · construction · power · timelines

The building was finished. The racks were in, the cooling loops were charged, and the commissioning team had signed off on every system except the one that mattered: the utility had not yet turned the power on, and its letter said the date was four years out. This is the normal condition of the data center business in 2026, not the exception. A developer can put up a 50 MW building in about eighteen months. Getting the grid to feed it takes more than four years. The gap between those two numbers is where projects live or die, and it is the reason the honest answer to "how long does it take to build a data center" is not a construction schedule. It is a power schedule with a building attached.

Keep the two clocks separate, because the industry quotes them separately and they have diverged. Time-to-build — sitework to a commissioned facility — runs 18 to 30 months in most published ranges, and JLL's global survey puts the average 50 MW build at 18 months (JLL, January 2026). Time-to-power — site control to the day the utility lets the load flow — runs more than four years on average in primary US markets on the same JLL measure, against 12 to 18 months before 2022. Add three to twelve months of pre-development and a greenfield site in a primary market is a three-to-six-year project. A site that already has power is a nine-to-fourteen-month project. The building did not get slower. The grid did.

Stage by stage: where the months go

Site selection comes first and is the fastest stage when it goes well: three to six months is typical from first screen to site control, six to twelve when the power question drags it out. The work is a feasibility study with the local utility — substation capacity, the cost of a new substation if there is none, water, fiber, and whether the land is already zoned for the use. Sites with industrial zoning and confirmed power capacity command a 20–30% premium over raw land, and that premium is the market pricing the rest of this article.

Permitting and entitlements run six to eighteen months on most published timelines — six to twelve typical, twelve to eighteen or more when the project needs a New Source Review air permit in a nonattainment county or draws organized opposition. Opposition is not a footnote. JLL's midyear 2026 report found 79% of Americans support US leadership in AI but only 14% support a data center in their own community — a 65-point gap that shows up at zoning hearings as the project's most unpredictable delay. Design and development run nine to eighteen months alongside permitting. Construction runs twelve to thirty-six months depending on size: a powered shell in twelve to eighteen, a turnkey 20 to 60 MW hall in about eighteen, a 100 to 300 MW campus phase in twenty-four to thirty-six. Commissioning — the five levels of testing from component to integrated systems — takes three to six months per hall. IT installation and ramp take two to four months per hall, xAI installed 100,000 GPUs at Colossus in 19 days — proof the IT stage is not the constraint when everything upstream is ready.

Energy procurement is listed last in most timelines and should be listed first. Negotiation takes six to twelve months — and then the queue takes over. The slippage data says the sequence is optimistic more often than not: 57% of data center projects slipped three months or more in 2025, according to JLL, and the average US equipment lead time reached 42 weeks, 83% longer than in 2019.

The queue: the stage that swallowed the schedule

Grid interconnection is a study process, not a purchase. The sequence runs application, screening, feasibility study, system impact study, facilities study, interconnection agreement, utility construction, and energization — and each study has a queue in front of it. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's (LBNL) Queued Up: 2026 Edition (July 2026) puts the median project at more than three years from interconnection request to agreement, and more than five years — 61 months — from request to commercial operation, for power projects completed in 2025. That study measures generation and storage projects, not data center loads, but it measures the same congested system, and the data center numbers are worse: CBRE reported waits above ten years in some constrained markets in mid-2026, and grid connection waits in major US hubs now stretch seven to ten years. New grid infrastructure itself takes five to fifteen years through planning, permitting, and construction. PJM, the grid operator covering 13 states and about a third of US electricity consumption, is still working through interconnection backlog from 2020 — a developer filing a new large-load request today would not see capacity auction eligibility until the early 2030s at best.

Texas is where the queue is largest and most legible. ERCOT's large-load interconnection queue stood at roughly 474 GW as of June 2026, about 90% of it data centers, according to ERCOT's testimony to the Texas Senate and Utility Dive's August 2026 reporting. That is more than five times ERCOT's record peak demand of 91.1 GW, set July 22, 2026. Oncor, the transmission utility for much of north Texas, reported roughly 282 GW of data center interconnection requests in its territory alone in its second-quarter 2026 results, up from 271 GW three months earlier. The queue is a waiting list for requests, most of which will never be built — which is why Texas spent 2026 building a filter.

Texas wrote the rulebook down

Under Senate Bill 6 and the Public Utility Commission of Texas rule adopted September 18, 2026 (16 TAC §25.194), loads of 75 MW or more pay a $100,000 study fee and post $50,000 per MW of security before study, with a 24-month energization grace period. On August 3, 2026, ERCOT paused its Batch Zero study — the one-time batch process sorting the large-load queue, allocating capacity dated 2028 to 2032 — along with final classification and energization approvals for computational loads of 75 MW or more, pending a state audit. Verification reports are due December 10, 2026, and ERCOT has said the Batch Zero study will not finish by its April 9, 2027 deadline. On September 21, 2026, the governor ordered the state environmental agency to halt issuance of all data center permits, including islanded behind-the-meter sites, until the ERCOT and water audits finish around mid-December 2026.

The 75 MW line is now the most important number in Texas site selection. A 60 MW project sits under it: outside Batch Zero, outside the energization pause, outside the $50,000-per-MW security. That is why the 20 to 60 MW bespoke AI facility has become a distinct product in 2026 — not because 60 MW is a natural size, but because 75 MW is a regulatory cliff. The timeline answer in Texas now depends on which side of that line the project falls, and developers are sizing projects to the rule rather than to the demand.

The equipment queue behind the power queue

Even with power secured, the building waits on factories. Generator step-up transformer lead times passed 160 weeks in early 2026, according to Wood Mackenzie data reported by Reuters — more than three years for a single component. GPU clusters run 36 to 52 weeks from order to rack. A dedicated substation and interconnect, once approved, is an 18-to-24-month build of its own. The International Energy Agency estimates that power constraints could delay about 20% of planned data center projects, and that almost half of all US data centers sit in just five regional clusters — the constraint is concentrated where the demand is concentrated.

The workarounds are real and they are priced like workarounds. About 50 GW of behind-the-meter data center power capacity was announced in 2025 alone, according to Cleanview's early-2026 report — developers building their own generation rather than waiting for the grid. Bridge power — engines, fuel cells, mobile turbines — can be in place in four to nine months at best and twelve to twenty-four typically, against thirty-six to forty-eight months for new frame turbines. Microsoft's twenty-year power purchase agreement with Constellation Energy covers 835 MW from the restarted Three Mile Island Unit 1 reactor, a deal signed in 2024 that reads, in hindsight, like a timeline hedge: buy the power plant, skip the queue. Epoch AI's tracking of the largest US AI campuses puts the time from start of construction to 1 GW of facility power at roughly one to 3.6 years — the campuses that move fastest are the ones that solved power before they solved construction.

The building is the part of the project the developer controls. The power is the part the queue controls. Every data center schedule is a bet on which of those two facts matters more, and in 2026 the queue is winning.

The honest counterargument

Not every project waits four years, and the projects that don't are the ones worth studying. Distribution-level service from a substation with spare capacity is the only grid path that still fits inside a construction schedule — six to twelve months at best, twelve to eighteen typical, for loads under roughly 10 to 20 MW. Small-format modular developers claim groundbreaking to in-service in as little as six months for standardized designs, with components ordered before permits issue. And the queue itself is partly speculative: 474 GW of requests against a 91.1 GW system peak means most requests are options, not projects, and the ERCOT audit now under way is designed to sort the real ones from the placeholders. When the sorting is done, the queue will shrink without a single megawatt being built.

The deeper challenge is that the building is genuinely getting faster while the narrative says everything is slow. Prefabrication, modular halls, and the Colossus example show the construction and IT stages compressing toward months. JLL counts 66 GW under construction in North America, 95% of it pre-committed — the industry is not standing still, it is building at a pace that would have been unimaginable five years ago. The counterargument is not that power is plentiful. It is that the constraint is specific — transmission-level interconnection in primary markets for loads above 75 MW — and that a project designed around the constraint, smaller, on existing substation capacity, or with its own generation, can still move at construction speed.

What this means for buyers and builders

Four conclusions follow. First, start power before land: the interconnection application should be filed before the site is bought, because the study clock is the longest clock on the project and it does not start until the application does. Second, size to the threshold — in Texas that means thinking hard before crossing 75 MW at a single site, and everywhere it means checking whether the load can be served at distribution voltage before assuming transmission. Third, budget bridge power as a line item, not a contingency: twelve to twenty-four months of temporary generation is the cost of a firm date, and the developers who treat it as optional are the ones announcing delays. Fourth, date every contract to energization, not completion — the renewal, the lease commencement, the GPU delivery, and the customer commitment should all key off the day the utility lets the load flow, because the building will be ready first and the building being ready first is the trap.

A data center takes eighteen to thirty months to build and three to six years to deliver, and the difference is the grid. The developers who buy powered land at a premium, file interconnection first, and size under the regulatory cliffs are not building faster. They are starting earlier on the only clock that matters.
