Water Cooling vs. Air Cooling: What the AI Rack Does to Operating Costs

An air-cooled rack tops out around 25 kilowatts. An AI rack draws 60 to 120. The cooling decision is not an efficiency choice — it is the decision that determines whether the facility can run AI workloads at all.

PRIME_DOMAIN_CAPITAL_RESEARCH · 2026-11-19 · cooling · liquid · operating costs

The operator had a building full of air-cooled racks, a waiting list of AI customers, and a problem that no amount of fans could solve. The customers wanted 60-kilowatt racks. The building was designed for 10. The air handlers could move more air, the chillers could run colder, and the hot aisle could be sealed tighter — and none of it mattered, because air is a bad coolant. At 60 kilowatts per cabinet, air cooling does not become expensive. It becomes impossible. The operator's choice was not between air and liquid. It was between liquid and no AI business at all.

The physics is the constraint, and the constraint is simple. Air carries very little heat per unit of volume, which means that cooling a high-density rack with air requires moving an enormous volume of air at high velocity, and the fans that move it consume power of their own. The industry's rule of thumb, formalized by ASHRAE Technical Committee 9.9, is that direct liquid cooling should be considered above 20 kilowatts per rack — and an AI rack draws 60 to 120 kilowatts per cabinet, three to six times that threshold. Air is feasible to roughly 12 to 25 kilowatts per rack, and beyond that the fan power, the ductwork, and the aisle temperatures make the design unworkable. The AI rack did not make liquid cooling attractive. It made air cooling irrelevant.

The efficiency gap, in numbers

The efficiency gap is measured in PUE — power usage effectiveness, the ratio of total facility power to IT power — and the gap is large. An October 2026 survey of 236 operators across 1,184 facilities, published by AI Industry Reviews, puts air-only cooling at a PUE of 1.52, rear-door heat exchangers at 1.44, direct-to-chip liquid cooling at 1.27, single-phase immersion at 1.12, and two-phase immersion at 1.08. The global average PUE, according to the Uptime Institute's 2023 survey, is 1.58 — meaning the average facility in the world wastes more than half as much power again on cooling and overhead as it delivers to the servers. Direct-to-chip cooling cuts that overhead by more than half. Immersion cuts it by more than two-thirds.

The operating-cost difference is what the PUE gap means in dollars. On a 50-megawatt site paying California electricity at $0.205 per kilowatt-hour, the cooling overhead of air cooling runs to about $35.9 million a year. Direct liquid cooling brings it to about $11.7 million. The difference — $24 million a year, every year — is the entire business case for liquid cooling, and it is larger than the capital cost of the cooling system itself. The capital costs run the other way: air cooling costs about $1.9 million per megawatt to build, rear-door heat exchangers $2.4 million, direct-to-chip $3.1 million, single-phase immersion $3.6 million, and two-phase immersion $4.4 million. Liquid cooling costs more to build and far less to run, and the payback depends on the rack density and the electricity price. Immersion breaks even above 50 kilowatts per rack and $0.10 per kilowatt-hour, with payback as low as 1.6 years at 80-plus kilowatts per rack. At AI densities, the payback is measured in months.

The ten-year math

The ten-year total cost of ownership tells the same story at cluster scale. For a 64-rack AI cluster, advanced air cooling costs about $42 million over ten years, at a PUE of 1.45 to 1.60. Direct-to-chip cooling costs about $31 million, at a PUE of 1.10 to 1.20. Single-phase immersion costs about $28 million, at a PUE of 1.03 to 1.08. The liquid-cooled cluster is a third cheaper over its life than the air-cooled one, and the gap widens as the rack density rises. Schneider Electric's own analysis, for a 2-megawatt deployment, puts air and liquid at near parity at 10 kilowatts per rack — $7.02 per watt for air, $6.98 for liquid — and shows liquid pulling ahead as density rises: $6.33 per watt at 20 kilowatts per rack, a 10 percent saving, and $6.02 per watt at 40 kilowatts per rack. The crossover is at the density the industry already passed. At 60 kilowatts and above, there is no crossover to debate.

Water use is the second operating cost, and it is the one that draws regulators' attention. Air cooling consumes about 1.71 liters of water per kilowatt-hour. Immersion cooling consumes 0.14 to 0.21 liters — an order of magnitude less. In regions where water is scarce or where the data center's water use is a political issue, the difference is not a footnote. It is the difference between a permit and a protest. The dielectric fluid itself is a cost worth naming: $25 to $45 per liter, accounting for 8 to 15 percent of immersion capital expenditure, with a lifetime of five to eight years. It is not free, and it is not negligible, but it is a fraction of the energy saving it enables.

Retrofit, and what it actually takes

The question operators actually ask is not whether liquid cooling is better. It is whether an existing air-cooled facility can be converted, and what the conversion costs in time and disruption. The answer depends on the technology. Rear-door heat exchangers — a liquid loop in the back door of the rack, with the rest of the room still air-cooled — retrofit in about five months, and they are the lowest-disruption path to liquid cooling. Direct-to-chip — cold plates on the processors, with the rest of the rack still air-cooled — takes about nine months. Single-phase immersion, which requires removing the racks and replacing them with tanks, takes about eleven months. Two-phase immersion takes about fourteen. The retrofit time is the real cost, because the facility is earning nothing from the racks that are being converted, and the conversion happens rack by rack, row by row, while the rest of the facility keeps running.

The counterargument is that liquid cooling is a trap — a capital-intensive bet on a technology that locks the operator into a single vendor's fluid, a single vendor's cold plates, and a single vendor's maintenance contract, in a market where the standards are still being written. The argument has force. The dielectric fluid market is concentrated, the cold-plate designs are proprietary, and an operator who builds for one vendor's immersion tanks cannot easily switch to another's. Air cooling, for all its inefficiency, is a commodity: any fan, any chiller, any contractor. The risk is real, and it is the reason the most common deployment is not immersion but direct-to-chip — a hybrid that keeps the air-cooled room, adds liquid where the heat is, and preserves the operator's ability to change course. Direct-to-chip is the compromise, and the compromise is winning: it is the technology the hyperscalers are deploying at scale, and it is the technology the retrofit market is built around.

An operator choosing between air and liquid should start with the rack density, not the brochure. Below 20 kilowatts per rack, air is fine, and the capital saving is real. Above 20 kilowatts, direct liquid cooling pays for itself in energy savings, and the payback shortens as density rises. Above 50 kilowatts, immersion becomes cheaper over ten years than direct-to-chip, and the water saving becomes a permitting advantage. And above 60 kilowatts — the density of the AI racks that every customer now wants — the choice is already made. The only question left is which liquid technology, and how fast the retrofit can be done without losing the customers who are waiting for the capacity.
