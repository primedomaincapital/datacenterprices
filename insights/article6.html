Data Center Construction Cost: What It Costs to Build a Data Center in 2026

Data center construction cost in 2026: about $11.3 million per MW for a standard build and $15–$20 million or more for AI-optimized, or roughly $505 per square foot — the full breakdown by tier, region, and cost driver.

PRIME_DOMAIN_CAPITAL_INSIGHTS · 2026-11-14 · data center construction cost

In July 2026, American developers started $84.1 billion of data center construction in seven months — nearly three times the spending of a year earlier — and the average project cost $1.06 billion, up from $352.8 million in 2025. That is the number that frames every other figure in this article. Data centers are rising in cost faster than they are growing in size, according to ConstructConnect’s September 2026 report, and the gap between a cheap build and an expensive one has widened into a range so broad that a single per-square-foot figure is useless without a tier, a market, and a definition of what is included.

The 2026 benchmarks: per megawatt and per square foot

Large facilities are priced per megawatt, not per square foot, and the per-megawatt benchmarks agree more than they disagree. JLL’s 2026 Global Data Center Outlook puts shell-and-core cost at $11.3 million per MW for a standard build, and $15–$20 million or more per MW for an AI-optimized one, before land and compute. Turner & Townsend’s 2026–27 Data Centre Construction Cost Index, built on a typical 60 MW design across 62 markets and more than 500 live or recent projects, puts the US average at $12.08 per watt — about $12.1 million per MW of construction cost — with the most advanced AI-specific facilities at $14–$16 per watt. Cushman & Wakefield’s 2026 guide puts the all-in greenfield cost at $17.6 million per MW once land and power infrastructure are counted, up 21% since late 2024.

Per square foot, the figures spread wider because they measure different buildings. Gordian’s RSMeans model, published by Building Design+Construction, puts a one-story computer data center at $505 per square foot nationally in Q1 2026, from $398 in Austin to $688 in Palo Alto. ConstructConnect’s tracking of 2026 starts puts the average at $818.20 per square foot, 57% above the comparable 2025 figure — but that average covers projects from under $300 to over $2,200 per square foot. Terrapin Construction Group’s 2026 guide ranges from $320 per square foot for an enterprise build to $1,280 for Tier IV. The honest reading: $500 a square foot buys a defined one-story building on paper; $800–$1,200 buys the redundant, liquid-cooled reality of a 2026 AI facility.

None of these numbers is wrong. They count different things — shell and core against all-in, a modeled building against started projects, a national average against a tier. The error is lifting one figure out of its definition and budgeting against it. A per-MW figure is the right unit for a hyperscale or colocation build; a per-square-foot figure is the right unit for a small enterprise facility. Mixing the two is how budgets miss by 40% — the same spread Turner & Townsend reports between the cheapest and most expensive US regions.

The cost breakdown: where the money goes

Electrical and power distribution is the largest line, at 20–28% of construction cost, followed by mechanical and cooling at 18–24%, and generators, UPS, and batteries at 10–16%. The rest is structure, fit-out, and fees. Those shares explain why AI changed the math: liquid cooling and high-density power push the two largest lines up at once, and an AI-optimized facility costs 50–100% more per square foot than a traditional one.

By tier, the 2026 ranges run: enterprise or Tier II at $600–$750 per square foot and $8–$10 million per MW; colocation or Tier III at $750–$950 and $10–$12 million; hyperscale or Tier IV at $950–$1,100-plus and $12–$15 million; AI-optimized at $1,100–$1,500-plus and $15–$20 million or more. Most projects land between $11.9 million and $20.6 million all-in, with a mid-range average near $15.2 million — the small end of the market, not the hyperscale end. A small data center, a server room or a single-hall enterprise build, lives at the bottom of those ranges; it is still a multi-million-dollar project, and its per-square-foot rate runs high because the power and cooling are concentrated into a small footprint.

What moves the number: region, power, and time

Region moves the number by up to 40%, by Turner & Townsend’s measure. Coastal metros and high-cost labor markets sit at the top of every range; Austin’s $398 against Palo Alto’s $688 in the RSMeans model is the same building priced in two labor markets. Power moves it more: a site that needs a substation, a transmission extension, or a 24–48 month grid connection carries infrastructure cost that never appears in a per-square-foot table. Time moves it fastest of all — construction costs rose 57% per square foot year over year in ConstructConnect’s 2026 starts data, and Cushman & Wakefield’s all-in figure is up 21% since late 2024. A quote from last year is a different project. Typical timelines run 12–24 months for a standard build and 18–36 months for the largest tiered facilities, and every month of delay reprices the job.

Build, buy, or rent: the decision the construction number forces

A construction budget is not a decision until it is compared with the alternatives. Colocation rents in primary markets average about $194.95 per kW per month, and an 8-GPU node costs roughly $406,000 over three years in colocation against about $720,000 on on-demand cloud — the full comparison is in our cost breakdown. Building only wins that comparison at sustained high utilization, with power secured and a 3–5 year horizon. Container and modular data centers sit outside the per-square-foot math entirely: they are priced per unit, chosen for speed of deployment rather than lowest cost, and make sense where a permanent build cannot wait for a grid connection. The construction number is the entry fee. The operating number — power at $0.058–$0.13 per kWh by market, staffing, refresh — is the bill that follows.

Budget per megawatt for a large build and per square foot for a small one, name the tier before naming the price, and re-price every quarter — because in a market where the average project tripled in a year, last year’s construction cost is not a benchmark.
