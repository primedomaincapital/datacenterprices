What Does a Megawatt of Data Center Capacity Cost in 2026?

A leased megawatt costs about $1.98 million a year at CBRE's H1 2026 large-block rate — and $2.46 million bought small. Why size, power, and vacancy set the price.

PRIME_DOMAIN_CAPITAL_RESEARCH · 2026-11-19 · pricing · megawatt · markets

The quote on the table said $204,690 a month. The quote on the next page said $165,310. Same megawatt, same country, same half of the same year. The buyer — a mid-size AI company shopping for its first wholesale block — assumed one of the two numbers was a mistake. Neither was. The first was the price of a megawatt bought in a 250-kilowatt slice. The second was the price of a megawatt bought as part of a 10-megawatt commitment. In 2026, the question "what does a megawatt cost" has no answer until you say how big the bite is, where the power comes from, and what the lease actually includes. The megawatt is not one product. It is a menu, and the spread between the cheapest and the most expensive item on it is the story of the whole market.

Start with the benchmark the industry actually quotes. CBRE's North America Data Center Trends H1 2026, published August 27, 2026, puts the average asking rent for a 250 to 500 kW requirement in the eight primary North American markets at $204.69 per kW per month, up 4.3% in six months from $196.25 at the end of 2025. The same report prices a 500 kW to 3 MW requirement at $183.75, up 7.9% in the half; a 3 to 10 MW requirement at $175.00, up 8.3%; and a 10 MW-and-up requirement at $165.31. CBRE expects the small-block average to pass $215 per kW per month before the year is out, according to its midyear review. Every size band rose. The larger bands rose fastest.

Turn those rates into the unit the question asks for. A megawatt at the small-block rate costs $204,690 a month, or about $2.46 million a year. A megawatt inside a 10 MW-plus commitment costs $165,310 a month, or about $1.98 million a year. A full 10 MW block at that rate runs about $1.65 million a month — just under $19.8 million a year — before a single kilowatt-hour of power is billed, because these are asking rents for space and committed power capacity, not electricity-inclusive quotes. That arithmetic is ours, from CBRE's published rates; the rates are CBRE's. The gap between the two megawatts is roughly $480,000 a year, every year, for five years if the term runs that long. That is the price of buying small.

Size is not the only multiplier. JLL's year-end 2025 North America schedule, quoted by JLL's Stuart Miller in an MBA NewsLink Q&A in October 2026, prices requirements under 250 kW at $334 per kW per month all-in — power included — against $201 plus electricity for 250 kW to 1 MW, $167 plus electricity for 1 to 5 MW, $156 for 5 to 20 MW, and $141 plus electricity above 20 MW. Read the two schedules together and the shape is unmistakable: the per-kilowatt price falls by more than half as the commitment grows from a cage to a campus. JLL's midyear 2026 report puts rents up nearly 70% since 2020, growing about 9% a year, with further growth expected through 2030.

Why Northern Virginia charges what it charges

Northern Virginia is the market where the price list meets a wall. CBRE's H1 2026 report counts 4,496.5 MW of inventory there — more than a gigawatt added in a year — and a vacancy rate of 0.2%, down from 0.7% a year earlier. Only 10.8 MW of capacity was available to lease. Net absorption ran 467.6 MW in six months, the most of any primary market, after a record 1,148.3 MW in the first quarter alone. There are 2,420.2 MW under construction, up 16.5% year on year, and every megawatt scheduled to deliver in 2026 was already committed, pushing preleasing into 2027 and beyond. CBRE estimates that fewer than 1,500 MW of everything under construction across the primary markets remains available — roughly six months of demand at the current absorption pace.

The asking band for Northern Virginia in H1 2026 ran $190 to $235 per kW per month for a 250 to 500 kW requirement, and $160 to $185 for 10 MW and up. Power there runs $0.07 to $0.10 per kWh in CBRE's market table — cheap by coastal standards, and beside the point. A megawatt running flat out consumes 8,760 megawatt-hours a year; at those power rates that is $613,000 to $876,000 a year for electricity alone, on top of rent. Dominion Energy, the region's main utility, said on its second-quarter 2026 call that about 12 GW of data center capacity is now under electric service agreements, with more than 53 GW in various stages of contracting — a pipeline more than four times the region's entire operating inventory. That is why the rent is what it is: the queue for power is longer than the queue for buildings.

The geography test: is the Midwest really half the price?

Ask around the industry and you will hear that the same megawatt costs twice as much in Northern Virginia as in the Midwest. The published numbers do not support that claim as stated — and the reason they don't is worth more than the claim itself. CBRE's eight primary markets include exactly one Midwestern market, Chicago, and its H1 2026 band for a 250 to 500 kW requirement is $200 to $230 per kW per month — a touch above Northern Virginia's $190 to $235, not half of it. Chicago's asking rents rose 14.7% year over year in the first quarter of 2026, the fastest of any primary market CBRE tracks. Geography, measured like for like, is not doing the work the saying assigns to it.

What is doing the work is size, structure, and availability. The two-times gap is real, but it runs between a small all-in requirement and a large wholesale one: JLL's $334 all-in for under 250 kW against $141 plus power above 20 MW is a multiple of more than two — before the power bill, which the small number includes and the large one does not. The genuine geographic discount lives outside the primary markets, in the frontier markets where the building is happening: JLL's midyear 2026 report puts 77% of all capacity under construction there, and Texas alone holds 26 GW against Virginia's 13 GW. The buyers paying half price are not buying the same megawatt in a cheaper city. They are buying a bigger megawatt, further from Ashburn, with power attached.

Lease structure: the price behind the price

An asking rent is an opening position, not a bill. Wholesale colocation in 2026 typically runs on three-to-five-year terms with annual escalations of 3% or more built in, according to JLL — which means a $165.31 rate signed today is a $185 rate by year five before any renewal negotiation. Operators commonly offer two to three months free on a new term, which cuts the effective first-year rate by roughly a sixth and then vanishes. Cross-connects, remote hands, and one-time setup sit outside the rent. Metered power sits outside it too, and in markets where the power rate is the negotiation, the rent is the distraction.

Even the benchmarks disagree in the details: CBRE's H2 2025 report printed its 250 to 500 kW average as both $195.94 and $196.25 in different sections, and this article follows the $196.25 pricing-section figure. A buyer who compares a $334 all-in quote with a $165 power-exclusive one and calls the difference a saving has made a $613,000-a-year error, at Northern Virginia power rates, on a single megawatt.

Build it instead? The other megawatt price

The lease price only makes sense against the alternative, and the alternative has its own 2026 price tag. Building a megawatt costs roughly $11.3 million for shell and core on JLL's 2026 forecast, and $17.6 million all-in on Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 cost guide — up 21% since late 2024 — before a single GPU is bought. The full breakdown is in our construction-cost analysis. A 10 MW build is a $113 million to $176 million project; the same 10 MW leased at CBRE's large-block rate is about $19.8 million a year in rent. The rough breakeven, ignoring power, financing, and the value of the land at the end, sits somewhere around six to nine years — which is why hyperscalers, who hold for decades, build, and why everyone else leases. JLL counts hyperscalers at 59% of 2026 tenant demand, with 28 GW of owner-occupied capacity under construction. The lease market is, increasingly, the market for everyone who is not a hyperscaler, and its prices are set by the hyperscalers' appetite for the same power.

Powered land is the tell. Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 guide puts powered land in primary US markets at an average of $584,000 per MW in 2026 to date, up 51% year on year. Land with power attached is appreciating faster than the buildings on it.

A megawatt without power is a warehouse with good wiring. The 2026 price of a megawatt is the price of a megawatt with power, in a market where someone still has some to sell.

The honest counterargument

Everything above assumes the demand forecasts are right, and they might not be. Rents up 70% since 2020, vacancy at 1.4% across CBRE's primary markets and 1% on JLL's measure for a third straight year, 95% of the 66 GW under construction already pre-committed — that is a market priced for demand that never pauses. If AI spending slows, the pre-commitments become the overhang: 66 GW is a great deal of capacity to absorb if the tenants' own forecasts were written in a boom. The four largest hyperscalers guide to roughly $730 billion of 2026 capital expenditure, and three of them ran negative free cash flow in the third quarter — the build is debt- and lease-funded, and leased capacity is the first thing a CFO renegotiates in a downturn.

There is also a simpler challenge: the benchmarks themselves are asking rents, and asking rents in a 0.2%-vacancy market are whatever the landlord says they are. Signed rents are private. The bands are wide — $190 to $235 in Northern Virginia for the same size band — and a real quote can land outside them. Singapore averages $403 per kW per month and Frankfurt $235 to $265 on CBRE's global figures, which means the US market, expensive as it feels, is not the ceiling. The counterargument does not say megawatts are cheap. It says the price is a forecast, and forecasts are the thing this market has been worst at.

What this means for buyers

Four conclusions follow, and they are all about buying differently rather than buying cheaper. First, buy size: the step from a 250 kW slice to a multi-megawatt commitment is worth roughly $480,000 a year per megawatt on CBRE's H1 2026 rates. Second, buy power before space — a cheap rent on a site that energizes in 2029 is the most expensive deal in the building. Third, price the escalation: a 3%-plus annual escalator adds roughly 16% to the year-one rate by the final year. Fourth, compare all-in cost per delivered megawatt-year, not headline rates — our data center cost calculator runs exactly that comparison.

The megawatt question has a real answer in 2026: about $2.46 million a year to lease small, about $1.98 million a year to lease large, and $11 million to $18 million to build — plus power, plus time. The buyers who get the best of it are not the ones who find the cheapest market. They are the ones who buy the biggest block, on the earliest power, and read the rent as what it is: a bet on a queue that is getting longer, in a market where the cheapest megawatt is the one that is already energized.
