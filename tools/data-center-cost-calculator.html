Price the power, not the rack

Colocation is sold by the kilowatt-month, and the per-kW rate is the number that decides the invoice. In 2026 the national average across primary North American markets is $194.95 per kW per month, according to CBRE — up 6.5% year over year, the fourth straight annual increase — while national vacancy sits at 1.4%, a historic low. At that vacancy, the rate on the quote is a starting point, not a price.

Two facts shape every result this page produces. First, asking rents are not signed rents: the figures below are what landlords publish, and operators do not publish what tenants actually sign. Second, the headline rate is the smallest line on the invoice. Broker analysis finds that power billing model, cross-connects, and remote hands add 25–30% to the real monthly cost, with the full range running 20–60% depending on connectivity. This calculator shows the headline rate and the loaded cost side by side, so the comparison is honest.

// METHODOLOGY

How the estimate is built

Monthly colocation cost is capacity in kW multiplied by the market's published per-kW-month range. The total over the term is the monthly figure multiplied by the number of billed months. The liquid-cooling toggle adds $45 per kW per month; the uplift toggle multiplies the monthly figure by 1.25 at the low end and 1.30 at the high end, matching the published 25–30% range; the free-months toggle deducts two billed months. The electricity comparison line is capacity × 730 hours × the published $/kWh range — 730 hours is a 24×7 month — and covers the IT load at full draw only, before cooling overhead. A typical PUE of 1.4 means the facility draws 1.4 MW to deliver 1 MW of compute, so the raw-electricity line understates the metered bill by design; it is shown for scale, not added to the total.

The build estimate is capacity in MW multiplied by JLL's published per-MW benchmark: $11.3 million for a standard build, $15–$20 million-plus for an AI-optimized one, both before land and compute. By tier, the published ranges run $8–$10 million per MW for Tier II, $10–$12 million for Tier III, $12–$15 million for Tier IV, and $15–$20 million-plus for AI-optimized — the calculator uses the two JLL headline benchmarks and leaves tier detail to the construction-cost article.

The honest limitation is the data itself. These are asking rents and published benchmarks, compiled from CBRE, JLL, Cushman & Wakefield, Turner & Townsend, Equinix, and operator and utility figures as reported in public benchmarks. Negotiated signed rents are not published by any operator, so no calculator can show them. A 135 kW rack at $200/kW-month is $27,000 a month in power alone — at that scale, a 10% difference in the power rate is worth more than most one-time concessions, and the only number that settles a negotiation is a quote on identical assumptions. Get three, on the same per-kW-month basis, with the same billing model, cross-connects, and cooling. Then re-run the math every renewal cycle: the average project cost tripled in a year, and last year's rate is not a benchmark.
